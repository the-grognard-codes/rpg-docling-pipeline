# Rifts Ultimate Edition: campaign rules analysis

**Scope.** This report is based on the supplied `Rifts - Ultimate Edition.md`, using the book's printed page numbers (not the OCR line positions) where its contents/index supplies them. The attribute table on p. 281 is visibly corrupted in the OCR, so its values below use Palladium's supplied [Attribute Bonus and Speed Chart](https://palladiumbooks.com/attribute-bonus-chart-and-speed-chart/). Everything else is a reading of this book, not a ruling imported from another edition or sourcebook.

## 1. Rules inventory: what is actually in this file

This is substantively the complete *Rifts Ultimate Edition* core book, pp. 1-375. It includes:

- The North America setting, Coalition States and campaign context (pp. 9-41, 229-274).
- Character generation: eight attributes and bonuses, low and superhuman attributes, S.D.C./Hit Points/M.D.C., random psionics, alignments, advancement, XP awards, O.C.C. tables, skills, and insanity (pp. 279-337).
- The player O.C.C.s: Combat Cyborg, Crazy, Cyber-Knight, Glitter Boy, Headhunter, Juicer, Merc Soldier, Robot Pilot; Body Fixer, City Rat, Cyber-Doc, Operator, Rogue Scholar, Rogue Scientist, Vagabond, Wilderness Scout; Elemental Fusionist, Ley Line Walker/Rifter, Mystic, Shifter, Techno-Wizard; Burster, Dog Boy, Mind Melter, Psi-Stalker; Dragon Hatchlings. It also includes Coalition Grunt, SAMAS Pilot, Military Specialist, and Technical Officer (pp. 43-158, 231-237).
- Psionic categories and individual power descriptions, ISP, saves, combat, and ley-line interaction (pp. 164-184, 366-367).
- Magic fundamentals, P.P.E., ley lines/Rifts, the core invocation list and spell descriptions, and Techno-Wizard construction (pp. 185-228, 129-137).
- Coalition equipment: armor, common gear, weapons, missiles, power armor and robots, plus vehicles (pp. 238-274).
- Full core combat: hand-to-hand, action economy, robot/power-armor combat, injury/recovery, M.D.C., ranged fire, missile combat, psychic combat, Horror Factor, and Perception (pp. 338-367).

### Explicitly outside the file

The book points readers elsewhere for more content, rather than including it: the *Rifts Book of Magic* (larger spell/item catalogue); *Bionic Sourcebook* (Slave-Borg); *Sourcebook One* and *Conversion Book One* (additional R.C.C.s); *World Book 11: Coalition War Campaign* and *Sourcebook 4: Coalition Navy* (additional Coalition O.C.C.s); *World Book 12: Psyscape* (more Master Psychics); *World Book 10: Juicer Uprising* (Juicer variants); *World Book 23: Xiticix Invasion* (Psi-Stalkers); and several regional books. Those are not evidence for a core-book ruling here.

## 2. Ambiguities, contradictions, and table rulings

### A. P.P. is dominant, but not universal

**Text.** The corrected chart gives P.P. a bonus to **strike, parry, and dodge**; p. 281 calls P.P. agility/reflexes. The combat rules say to combine all applicable bonuses by *category* (p. 344). Ranged combat expressly says P.P. and hand-to-hand bonuses **do not** apply to modern weapons, but P.P. and an O.C.C. dodge bonus are the only bonuses for dodging gunfire (pp. 359-361).

**Competing readings.** A broad reading makes P.P. the bonus to every combat die. A narrow reading treats it as a melee/general strike-parry-dodge modifier, while W.P. is the offensive modifier for guns.

**Official table ruling.** Use the narrow reading. Apply P.P. to ordinary melee strike/parry/dodge and to a dodge of gunfire/energy. Do not apply it to firearm/missile **strike**, initiative, damage, skills, saving throws, or a roll where a power states its own bonus. Use only bonuses labelled for that maneuver; never pool “combat bonuses” into every roll. This honors both the attribute chart and the explicit modern-weapons exclusion.

**House rule — Medium.** Cap the P.P.-derived portion of any roll at +4 (P.P. 22); retain O.C.C., H2H and W.P. bonuses. *Consequence: extreme agility remains valuable but does not turn a 5+ M.D.C. hit threshold into a near-certainty at high level.*

### B. Gunfire dodge range penalties make close fire almost noninteractive

**Text.** A defender who knows of and sees the shooter may dodge; they must equal or beat the strike roll. At 10 feet the dodge is -10, and at 50 feet it is -5 (p. 361). The shooter needs only 8+ including W.P./Sniping bonuses (p. 360). The book does not say whether the range bands use the muzzle, target, or nearest edge, nor does it distinguish a careful shot from spray in the dodge penalty.

**Competing readings.** Strictly, a point-blank defender suffers -10 even while moving behind cover; others waive it because “dodging bullets” really means evading the shooter’s aim.

**Official table ruling.** Measure from shooter to target at the moment of the shot. Apply the printed -10/-5 only to a true dodge in open space; it does not remove the normal option to get behind actual cover before the shot. Awareness and line of sight remain prerequisites.

**House rule — High.** Replace -10/-5 with -4 within 10 ft. and -2 within 50 ft.; a defender with no cover still cannot dodge an unseen attack. Do not permit a dodge against an aimed called shot from point-blank unless the defender has automatic dodge. *Consequence: guns stay terrifying, but defensive movement is a real choice instead of an almost automatic failed die roll.*

### C. Four-missile volleys: “impossible to dodge” is narrower than it sounds

**Text.** A volley is two or more simultaneous missiles, one melee action and one strike roll (p. 362). The book says one to three guided missiles can be dodged, but “it is impossible to dodge a volley of four or more”; immediately after, it says that volley strikes every time **“as long as the attack was successful”** (p. 363). Dodging a missile gets out of the direct impact, not necessarily its blast radius; cover, roll with impact, blocking with robot arms, and shooting missiles down remain available (pp. 362-364).

**Competing readings.** “Impossible” is sometimes read as automatic damage with no attack roll, or as no defensive option at all. The next paragraph contradicts both: the volley still needs its one hit roll, and later paragraphs list mitigation.

**Official table ruling.** Roll once to strike the whole volley. On a miss, all miss; on a successful 4+ volley, no ordinary dodge cancels the direct hits. The target may still use cover, roll with impact, sacrifice locations, or shoot missiles. Resolve blast radius separately.

**House rule — High.** Replace the absolute rule with one *saturation dodge*: 4+ missiles impose -4 to the dodge; success avoids direct hits but never avoids blast damage unless the mover reaches cover/outside the radius. *Consequence: missile racks remain the area-denial apex, but a skilled/mobile PC is not deleted by a single all-or-nothing rule.*

### D. Action economy: dodge debt, “back-and-forth,” and initiative

**Text.** A melee is 15 seconds; initiative is rolled once each melee (p. 339). A trained character parries automatically and spends no action. A dodge, entangle, or roll with impact spends one action; automatic dodge does not (pp. 340-341, 344). Combat then alternates attackers; after someone has spent all attacks, they may parry incoming attacks, but a dodge “will take away one of his attacks from the next melee round” (pp. 341-342). A gunshot/short burst/pulse is one attack; long bursts/machine guns may consume more (p. 361).

**Competing readings.** Some tables treat all characters as taking every action in initiative order, while others take the prose literally and alternate individual attacks. Some disallow a dodge once the defender is out of actions; the text explicitly permits it, but makes it debt next melee.

**Official table ruling.** Track a current-melee action pool. Resolve one action/counteraction exchange at a time, in initiative order among the participants; a trained parry is free. Deduct dodge/entangle/roll immediately if possible. If the defender has zero actions, allow a normal dodge and mark one action debt for the next melee, as the text expressly says. Cap total debt at the character's normal next-melee action pool; after that, they may parry or accept the hit, but cannot take additional ordinary dodges. At next initiative, subtract debt before taking actions. Automatic dodge and parry never create debt.

**House rule — High (clarification, no mechanical change).** Put an action tracker at the table: `actions remaining | dodge debt next melee | free parries | auto dodges`. No action can be spent twice; declaring an aimed/called shot reserves its two/three actions up front, and an interrupted spell loses its reserved actions but not P.P.E. *Consequence: this removes the recurring timing argument and makes extra attacks matter exactly as the book says.*

### E. Spell timing and interruption are under-specified

**Text.** Levels 1-5 take one action; 6-10 two; 11-15 three (p. 189). Mid/high spells fail if the caster parries, dodges, strikes, runs, or otherwise breaks concentration; P.P.E. is not spent until completion (pp. 189-190). It does not say whether a caster who has begun a two-action spell may be interrupted in the gap or how their reserved actions appear in the back-and-forth sequence.

**Official table ruling.** A multi-action spell requires consecutive, declared casting actions. Put a “casting” marker on the character after the first; any hit, defense, movement, or other action before the last ends the attempt. The caster may choose to wait for consecutive slots rather than begin. No P.P.E. is lost; all committed actions are.

**House rule — Medium.** A caster may maintain an uninterrupted spell while using a **free trained parry only**, but not dodge, entangle, roll, attack, or move more than a step. *Consequence: a guarded mage can function without erasing the book’s stated vulnerability to a full press.*

### F. M.D.C. has a severe all-or-nothing overkill cliff

**Text.** One M.D. equals 100 S.D.C.; 1-99 S.D.C. cannot affect M.D.C. (p. 288). The last M.D.C. of any armor absorbs *all* damage from that attack, even 3 M.D.C. against a 21-M.D. plasma bolt; only a subsequent attack reaches the wearer (pp. 288, 355). M.D.C. has no A.R.; a hit either lands or is stopped by parry/dodge (pp. 339-340).

**Competing readings.** The printed rule is unequivocal, but it yields the counterintuitive result that 1 M.D.C. can stop an arbitrarily large hit. Tables often mistakenly carry excess through because it feels natural.

**Official table ruling.** In core-only play, apply the last-point rule exactly. Keep the “armor destroyed” event visible: the next M.D. hit is against an unprotected S.D.C. body unless another defense is active.

**House rule — Medium/High depending on campaign lethality.** When a single hit destroys personal M.D.C. armor, transfer excess at **one-tenth M.D. as S.D.C.** to the wearer (round down; a 21-M.D. hit through 3 M.D.C. deals 1 S.D.C., not 1,800). Vehicles/robots use the printed rule. *Consequence: armor condition matters before it reaches zero without making every armor breach instant death.*

### G. P.P.E./I.S.P. costs are fixed labels, not a point-buy formula

**Text.** Each spell description supplies its P.P.E. cost; the book says costs run from 1 to 2,000 and that the expensive spells “tend” to have spectacular effects (pp. 185-186). It similarly assigns a listed ISP cost to each psychic power, while use normally costs one action (pp. 164-184, 366). The book never derives cost from damage, range, duration, save, or utility. Ley lines add 10/20 P.P.E. each melee (20/40 for Line Walkers), while psychics receive 1D6+1 transient ISP each melee on a ley line (pp. 186, 366).

**Competing readings.** A literal reading treats the printed cost as immutable. A “common sense” reading re-prices individual powers, but has no common measuring stick and can punish a player after character selection.

**Official table ruling.** Printed costs are rules data, not invitations to case-by-case negotiation. Use the action, duration, line-of-sight, save, and concentration clauses as the actual balancing levers. Do not convert P.P.E. to I.S.P. except where the text expressly permits it (notably Techno-Wizard activation at double ISP).

**House rule — Medium.** Use an **escalation cost** for repeatedly casting the same offensive spell/power in one melee: base cost, then +50% (round up) for each additional use; reset next melee. Exempt healing, senses, and purely defensive upkeep. *Consequence: it limits a single underpriced combat effect without re-writing the spell lists or penalizing broad magical play.*

### H. Natural 20, modified 20, and stacking need a hard table convention

**Text.** A natural 20 always hits/criticals and only a natural 20 defense stops it; a 1-4 strike always misses (pp. 339-340). Critical damage adds bonuses before doubling/tripling (p. 341). The rules say applicable bonuses are cumulative but each maneuver has its own category (p. 344).

**Official table ruling.** “Natural” always means the unmodified d20 face. A modified 20 is neither automatic nor critical unless an ability independently expands critical range. Apply all legal bonuses once, to their labelled category, then resolve critical multipliers.

**House rule — Low.** Cap total *non-P.P.* strike, parry, and dodge bonuses at +10; class features that grant automatic dodge are unaffected. *Consequence: preserves specialty while keeping opposed rolls meaningful at high level.*

## 3. Character balance analysis

### The system’s real balance axis

There is no single “point value” or intended equal combat tier in the text. The book deliberately puts a doctor, a Glitter Boy, a dragon, and a Mind Melter in the same player menu. Balance therefore depends on campaign access to M.D.C. armor/weapons, ley lines, downtime, legal/Coalition pressure, and objectives. A standard human with light armor is extremely fragile in an M.D. encounter; the text itself calls the PCs the high-tech/specialized exception (p. 238) and describes an M.D. armor-and-rifle user as roughly a light APC (p. 356).

The useful comparison is capability band, not “which class wins a duel.” Ratings below assume first-level starting packages and ordinary field access, not unlimited Black Market purchases.

| Band / O.C.C.s | Combat & survival | Skills / equipment | Balance read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Augmented combat:** Combat Cyborg, Headhunter, Merc Soldier, Robot Pilot | Borg/Headhunter gain augmented body/strength; Merc has broad military options; Robot Pilot’s platform can dominate but is mission/logistics dependent. | Best conventional weapons, W.P.s, armor, vehicle/robot access. | Generally coherent if vehicles are scarce enough to matter. Robot Pilot and Glitter Boy swing wildly with starting platform access. |
| **Extreme combat:** Crazy, Juicer, Cyber-Knight, Glitter Boy | Crazy/Juicer get auto-dodge and large physical packages; Cyber-Knight has a renewable M.D. Psi-Sword and potentially major/master psionics; Glitter Boy starts with 770 main-body M.D.C. and Boom Gun. | Crazy/Juicer have broad skills and M.D. gear; Knight has fewer guns but strong supernatural/magic access; GB is gear-concentrated. | These outpace ordinary Men at Arms in action economy/survival. The Juicer’s finite life and Crazy’s escalating insanity are role-play prices, not encounter-balance prices. |
| **Civilian specialist:** Body Fixer, Cyber-Doc, Operator, Rogue Scholar, Rogue Scientist, City Rat, Vagabond, Wilderness Scout | Low intrinsic durability; Body Fixer/Cyber-Doc/Operator have small defensive bonuses. Scout is the strongest field survivor; City Rat is situational. | Their niche skill value is high: medicine, bionics, repair, science, teaching, infiltration, survival. | They are *undertuned in combat* only if every scenario is an M.D. firefight. Operators and medical roles shine only when repair/injury, investigation, and resource constraints are actually played. |
| **Mages:** Elemental Fusionist, Ley Line Walker/Rifter, Mystic, Shifter, Techno-Wizard | Low armor and poor pressed-melee resilience; enormous breadth, control, travel, and utility. Ley Line Walker gets 12 starting spells and highest mortal P.P.E.; TW can create permanent gear. | Strong knowledge; armor restrictions and casting interruption are intended brakes. | Off ley lines, generally powerful but manageable. On ley lines/nexus, Line Walker/Rifter and some spells scale dramatically. TW has long-term campaign-economy risk. |
| **Psychics:** Burster, Dog Boy, Mind Melter, Psi-Stalker | Burster is focused offense; Dog Boy/Psi-Stalker are capable scouts/soldiers; Mind Melter has Force Field, broad powers, and strong saves. | Moderate gear; Mind Melter’s powers often replace equipment. | Mind Melter is the standout human psychic: 12 selected powers at level 1 across all categories plus four fixed powers, 3D6x10+M.E. ISP, and strong bonuses (pp. 150-151). |

They need a separate campaign premise or compensating narrative restrictions. |
| **Coalition O.C.C.s** | Grunt/Military Specialist/Technical Officer are equipment-and-chain-of-command dependent; SAMAS Pilot begins at a high platform tier. | Superior issued logistics, but command may restrict access and mission choice (pp. 231-237). | Treat military support and command as real costs; otherwise SAMAS Pilot outclasses conventional combatants in open battles. |

### Specific over- and under-tuning

1. **Juicer — overtuned in an ordinary adventuring window.** It gets +2 attacks, +4 initiative, automatic dodge from all directions, enormous speed/endurance, augmented P.S., major S.D.C./HP, saves, and fast healing (pp. 78-80). The five-year death sentence is narratively severe but often irrelevant to a campaign’s encounter horizon. The automatic dodge also bypasses the action-economy cost that is supposed to control defense.

2. **Crazy — nearly Juicer-level combat plus a deeper skill package.** It has 3D6x10 S.D.C., 5D6 HP, augmented P.S., minimum P.P. 17, an extra attack, automatic dodge even from surprise/rear, heightened senses, minor psionics, and broad related skills (pp. 55-56). Insanity may create great stories, but it is GM-facing and inconsistent as a mechanical counterweight.

3. **Cyber-Knight — a high variance class whose random psychic result can invalidate niches.** Every Knight has a no-cost, indefinitely maintained 1D6 M.D. Psi-Sword that adds 1D6 at levels 3/6/9/12/15, shield, meditation and baseline combat bonuses. Seventy percent also receive psychic packages, including a 10% master result with super powers (pp. 63-66). A Master Psychic Knight can crowd out both melee and psychic roles without paying a comparably concrete price.

4. **Glitter Boy/SAMAS/Robot platforms — platform math beats character math.** The Glitter Boy’s 770 main body M.D.C. plus a Boom Gun is an immense first-level floor (pp. 71-73); SAMAS adds 300-mph flight, 250 main-body M.D.C. and a 1D4x10 rail-gun burst (pp. 240-243). The book’s “last M.D.C. absorbs all” rule further magnifies each platform’s remaining point. Terrain, stealth, ammo, repair, political visibility, and inability to bring the platform everywhere must be active constraints.

5. **Mind Melter — the best generalist controller.** It begins with four fixed powers plus 12 choices across all categories, including three Super powers, then adds four more at second level; its Force Field and thought-speed actions reduce the usual equipment dependency (pp. 150-151, 366). It is less durable without powers, but that weakness can disappear if the group treats powers as free to activate or allows unlimited rest/ley-line recovery.

6. **Dragon Hatchlings — campaign-breaking beside human O.C.C.s.** Even the less extreme types start with hundreds of M.D.C., regeneration, supernatural damage, exceptional stats, flight/teleport/metamorphosis or equivalent movement, and psychic packages. Royal Frilled starts as a Master Psychic with 12 normal powers, two Super powers, M.D.C. 1D6x100 +10/level, and regeneration (pp. 156-163). This is a different protagonist category, not merely a strong class.

7. **Operator, Body Fixer, Cyber-Doc, Rogue Scholar/Scientist, and Vagabond — scenario-dependent rather than bad.** Their text gives excellent repair, medical, bionic, scholarly, and teaching niches, but a light M.D.C. suit and limited guns do not buy enough agency in a chain of battles. The Operator’s repair/upgrade role is particularly valuable only if gear damage, ammunition, travel, and downtime are adjudicated (pp. 87-98).

### Scaling and math failures

- **Accuracy grows faster than defense against M.D.C. targets.** M.D.C. requires only 5+ to hit, whereas legal P.P., H2H, O.C.C., W.P., and sensor bonuses accumulate. At high bonus totals a combatant rarely misses; firearm defense is additionally penalized at close range. The real defense becomes auto-dodge, cover, action denial, or raw M.D.C., not an opposed roll.
- **Automatic dodge is a multiplicative action-economy advantage.** It preserves all attacks while ordinary dodge consumes present or next-round actions. A Juicer/Crazy therefore gains both offense and defense, not merely a defense bonus.
- **M.D.C. produces two discontinuities.** 1-99 S.D.C. does nothing, then 100 S.D.C. becomes 1 M.D.; and the last M.D.C. absorbs unlimited overkill. Small numerical changes can have no effect until suddenly decisive.
- **Volley damage scales linearly but defense stops at four missiles.** One hit roll can turn four or more missiles into four or more full damage packets. Adding one missile changes the defender’s basic option from an opposed check to none.
- **Ley lines change resource economies by melee, not encounter.** A normal mage draws P.P.E. every 15 seconds; Line Walkers draw double; psychics convert a transient 1D6+1 ISP every melee. A fight on a line is not a small terrain benefit; it can change the number of meaningful castings by an order of magnitude (pp. 186, 366).
- **Techno-Wizard construction can outscale the economy.** The rules provide formulas for construction and activation, but “which spells are required” and limitations are GM judgment; armor/vehicles are merely advised to have no more than four Functions (pp. 129-137). With downtime and components, one character can turn the party’s gear into a spell catalogue.

## 4. Recommended house-rule package, prioritized

Adopt the following as a package only if the group wants a more tactical, less swingy game. Each recommendation is concrete; do not combine a change with an opposite “common sense” exception at the table.

| Priority | Rule | Concrete fix | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | Action ledger | Use the current-actions/dodge-debt procedure in ruling D; reserve multi-action shots/spells before the first action. | Slightly more bookkeeping, much less timing litigation. |
| High | Gunfire dodge | Use -4 at 10 ft., -2 at 50 ft., awareness/LOS required; no point-blank dodge against aimed called shots without auto-dodge. | Mobile targets live longer, but guns retain a strong short-range edge. |
| High | Missile saturation | Four+ missiles impose -4 to dodge; success avoids direct impact only; blast/cover still matters. | Reduces one-roll character deletion while keeping launcher salvos decisive. |
| High | Auto-dodge gate | Auto-dodge works as printed, but each automatic dodge after the first in a melee is -2 cumulative until next melee. | Tones down Juicer/Crazy dominance without deleting their signature ability. |
| Medium | P.P. cap | P.P. contributes at most +4 to strike/parry/dodge; no P.P. firearm strike bonus. | Exceptional P.P. has less linear payoff. |
| Medium | M.D.C. breach | Personal armor passes one-tenth of excess M.D. as S.D.C. after zero; vehicles/robots retain printed last-point protection. | Armor attrition becomes meaningful, but breach is not automatically fatal. |
| Medium | Resource repetition | Repeated offensive spell/power in one melee costs +50% per additional use; reset next melee. | Less “same best blast” spam; more tracking. |
| Medium | Ley-line encounter budget | At a ley line, each caster may draw ambient energy only **once per melee**, as printed, and all unused supplemental energy vanishes at melee end; announce it at the start of each melee. | Enforces an easily missed limiter and keeps a line from becoming a vague infinite pool. |
| Medium | Techno-Wizard approval | Every new Function requires an agreed spell chain and one explicit limitation; maximum four functions on armor/vehicles is mandatory, not advisory. | More design review; protects party niches and campaign economy. |
| Low | Bonus ceiling | Cap non-P.P. strike/parry/dodge bonus at +10; natural 20 rules remain untouched. | Narrows high-level bonus escalation. |
